# Critique: Saturation law (T4): "More tasks, no gain: the data multiplier and saturation law of harness learners"

Reviewer stance: skeptical area chair and related-work searcher. Date: 2026-11-12.
Verdict: **borderline, park unless reframed (about 61/100).** No kill flag fires cleanly. But the headline ("more tasks don't help harness learners; selection beats adding") was published in Oct 2026 by **SSR 2610.28815**, with a mechanism ("noisy, redundant evidence fed to the optimizer") that is close to the proposed "evidence bandwidth" story. The positive side of bandwidth (more rollouts per round improves held-out success) is in **Scale and Selection 2609.39304**. "Self-generated ≈ curated" is in **DAEDALUS 2610.08048**, and a synthetic-vs-human substitution rate is in **2606.03800** (for RLVR). What is left open is a **quantitative law**, n\*(k), tested by *causal* manipulation of bandwidth in a world with a **known latent rule graph**. It should report rule-level coverage and seen-vs-held attribution, and say when generated tasks are worth real ones (coverage, not count). The paper has to be built around that law, or it reads as a toy-world replication of SSR.

## 3. Mock NeurIPS review

**Summary.** The paper studies how held-out performance of frozen-weight "harness learners" (GEPA, ACE-style memory, Meta-Harness-lite) scales with the number of real training tasks n and self-generated tasks m at matched rollout budget. It uses a procedural τ-style world with a known latent rule graph, varies evidence bandwidth k (failures per update step), and reports S_real, S_gen, n_eff, per-rule coverage and a seen/held split via fresh-instance re-serving. It claims saturation at a few dozen tasks, set by k rather than generator quality.

**Strengths**
- It asks a clean, practically important question (how much data does a self-improving harness need, and is synthetic data worth anything?) with a controlled design: matched rollouts, ≥5 seeds, several evolvers, open models.
- The known latent rule graph is the right instrument. It is the only way to tell *coverage* from *noise* from *redundancy*, which SSR cannot do on SWE-bench.
- The seen-vs-held attribution via fresh-instance re-serving is a valuable rigor contribution in the "Rethinking Eval" tradition.
- It is cheap and reproducible: the procedural generator and open models make a reusable artifact.

**Weaknesses**
1. **The headline is not new.** "More tasks, no gain" and "selection beats adding" are SSR's Observation 1 and 2, and SSR also fixes them. A reviewer will write: *"The authors rediscover SSR's paradoxical-degradation finding in a toy world. What does this add?"* The only defensible answer is a *quantitative, causal* law (n\* as a function of k) that SSR's correlational evidence cannot give.
2. **The mechanism is half-published.** Scale and Selection shows that more rollouts per round improves held-out success and that small batches overfit. SSR blames noisy, redundant evidence. Harness Evolution as Learning gives a coverage–estimation bound. "Evidence bandwidth sets n\*" therefore risks being the union of three prior observations. It is novel only if (i) k is manipulated *independently* of n and of total rollouts, and (ii) n\* shifts predictably (dose–response) as k changes.
3. **External validity of the toy world.** A procedural τ-style world with a crisp rule graph may saturate *because* it has few rules. n\* ≈ "number of rules / rules exposed per step" could be an artifact of world size. Reviewers will ask for (a) a sweep of rule-graph size R, showing n\* scales with R/k and not just k, and (b) at least one real benchmark (τ²-retail, AppWorld) where the law's prediction is checked.
4. **Confounds in "matched rollouts."** Larger n at fixed rollouts means fewer repeats per task, so variance-reduction effects (repeats denoise outcomes) are confounded with coverage. Larger k means longer reflection contexts, so the optimizer's long-context degradation is confounded with bandwidth. Both need explicit arms: repeats-per-task at fixed n, and k with context-length padding or a summarization control.
5. **Self-generated arm.** "Generated tasks are too easy" is now an expected null (DAEDALUS calibrates difficulty and nearly matches curated tasks; 2606.03800 reports 64% "too_easy" rejections). The informative variant is a generator that is *rule-coverage aware* vs *blind*. If n_eff only rises when generated tasks expose uncovered rules, that is a crisp finding. A static/adaptive/anchored sweep alone is incremental.
6. **Overspecified claim.** "A few dozen", "n_eff/m ≥ 1 only below n\*" and "most gains are S_seen" are four claims in one sentence. If any fails, the narrative frays. Pick one headline law and make the others corollaries.
7. **Scale of the grid.** 3 evolvers x 2 models x ~8 n values x 4 generated arms x several k x 5 seeds is roughly 2,000+ evolution runs. That is feasible on a cheap open model only if each run is small. The plan needs a power analysis and a fractional design.

**What makes it "workshop"**: one evolver, no k manipulation, the result "S_real plateaus at n≈30 and generated tasks help a bit". **"Engineering"**: building the procedural world and reporting curves without a law or a prediction. **Main-track**: a falsifiable scaling law S(n, k, R) fitted on the toy world that *predicts* n\* on a real benchmark out of sample, plus a demonstration that SSR/Curator-style selection works *because* it raises effective coverage per step (a unifying explanation of 3 to 4 recent methods).

**Likely score as proposed**: 4 (borderline reject); confidence 4. **With fixes**: 5 to 6.

## 5. Fixes (concrete, ordered by impact)
1. **Make the law the paper.** Pre-register S_held(n, k, R) ≈ f(coverage(n, k, R)). Prediction: n\* ∝ R / (k·ρ), where R is the number of latent rules and ρ is rules exposed per failure. Manipulate **k independently** (failures per reflection step: 1, 4, 16, 64) at fixed n and fixed total rollouts. Manipulate **R** (rule-graph size: 8, 32, 128). The falsifiable test is whether the saturation point moves linearly with R/k across all 3 evolvers.
2. **Out-of-sample check on a real benchmark.** Fit the law on the procedural world. Then predict n\* for GEPA on τ²-retail or AppWorld from measured k and an estimated rule count (e.g., distinct failure-pattern clusters, as in ActiveSaddler's arms), and check it. This is the step that turns "toy study" into "general insight".
3. **Unify the recent methods.** Run SSR-style evidence ranking, GEPA-Curator-style task curation and ActiveSaddler-style failure-pattern bandits as arms. Show that their gains are explained by *effective new-rule coverage per step*: same coverage gives the same gain regardless of method. That converts the three scoop papers into supporting evidence for your law.
4. **Replace the static/adaptive/anchored generator sweep with a coverage-aware vs coverage-blind generator** (an oracle generator that targets uncovered rules, an LLM generator told the current harness's failures, and a blind generator). Claim: n_eff/m depends on uncovered-rule exposure, not on validity or difficulty. Report the 2606.03800 trade rate ρ for comparability.
5. **Control the confounds**: repeats-per-task at fixed n (denoising vs coverage), and context-length-matched k (pad or summarize to equalize tokens). Report Agent-Plasticity-style gain per $ as a secondary axis.
6. **Seen vs held at rule level.** For each accepted edit, tag which rules it mentions. Re-serve fresh instances of seen-rule tasks vs tasks requiring unseen rules or rule compositions. That gives "S_seen vs S_held" a mechanistic meaning beyond 2609.39148's retention metric.
7. **Mentor hook (raises R).** Add one self-referential arm: a Gödel-Agent-style meta-agent that may edit its *own* k, batch selection or curriculum policy. Does it discover the bandwidth law, i.e. raise k or select for coverage? Even a negative result ("self-modifiers do not discover their own data bottleneck") is interesting.
8. **Prune the grid.** 2 evolvers (GEPA plus one memory or code evolver) x 1 cheap open model for the full surface, with the second model only at 3 n values. Use a fractional factorial over (n, k, R) and 5 seeds on the key cells. Do a power analysis from the $20 pilot.

## 6. Sharpened headline claim
"For frozen-weight harness learners, held-out gain is governed by **how many distinct latent rules the optimizer is shown per update**, not by how many tasks exist. The saturation point scales as n\* ∝ R/k (rules over failures-per-step) across evolvers. Beyond n\*, extra tasks buy only seen-instance gains. Self-generated tasks are worth real ones exactly to the extent that they expose rules the current harness has not covered. SSR, curation and adaptive curricula all work by raising this per-step coverage."
